# Humanize Copy — Meta-Patterns & Decision Records

The reasoning behind the kill-list and rules, so you can handle tells the list doesn't
name. Independent synthesis of widely-documented observations about AI writing
(2025–2026 editor/SEO discourse). Credited ideas, original expression — not a copy.

## Meta-patterns

### MP-1 · AI prose is the average of mediocre training text
Models default to the statistical mean of their corpus — millions of corporate
whitepapers and SEO posts. That mean is formal, hedged, buzzword-heavy, and tidily
structured. **Consequence:** the tells aren't random; they're the *most probable*
choices. Humanizing = deviating from probable toward specific.

### MP-2 · Specificity is the strongest human signal
The average AI sentence is generically true ("we help businesses grow"). Humans write
from particular experience: numbers, names, dates, grainy details. **Consequence:**
when unsure how to humanize a line, don't restyle it — *make it more specific*. A real
detail can't be generated from a vague prompt, so it reads as human.

### MP-3 · Neutrality is a tell; stance is human
RLHF pushes models to be balanced and harmless, so they avoid commitment.
**Consequence:** taking a clear position is both more human and more authoritative.
Hedges and both-sides framing are the linguistic fingerprint of the safety training.

### MP-4 · Rhythm carries as much signal as words
Low burstiness (uniform sentence length/shape) reads as machine even with clean
vocabulary. **Consequence:** edit the *cadence*, not just the word choice — vary
length, use fragments, break the rectangle.

### MP-5 · Plainer is more human than fancier
The instinct to "sound less AI" by elevating vocabulary backfires into syntax salad.
Human writing is plain and direct. **Consequence:** edits should usually *reduce*
syllable count and clause complexity, not increase it. Cut before you embellish.

## Decision records

### DR-1 · Replace buzzwords with specifics, not synonyms
- **Why (MP-1/MP-2):** a synonym keeps the emptiness; a specific carries information.
- **Apply:** "optimize workflows" → "cut your reporting time in half", not "enhance
  workflows".

### DR-2 · Cut the first 10%
- **Why (MP-1):** models open with probable filler ("In today's…"); the real claim is
  usually paragraph two.
- **Apply:** delete the warm-up; start on tension or a concrete claim.

### DR-3 · Vary the cadence on purpose
- **Why (MP-4):** uniform length is a detectable signal and a soporific read.
- **Apply:** no 3+ same-length runs; pair a long sentence with a short punch.

### DR-4 · Commit to a stance
- **Why (MP-3):** balanced/hedged copy reads as machine and lacks authority.
- **Apply:** give the recommendation; cut "it's important to note" / "aims to".

### DR-5 · Pass the pub test
- **Why (MP-5):** spoken-aloud naturalness catches both buzzwords and over-correction.
- **Apply:** read the worst paragraph aloud; if you wouldn't say it to a colleague,
  rewrite it simpler.

### DR-6 · Human sandwich, never raw output
- **Why (MP-1/MP-2):** the human must supply the angle and the specifics the model
  can't.
- **Apply:** human strategy → AI draft → human edit (cut tells, add specifics, verify
  facts). Never publish step two.

### DR-7 · Match register to medium
- **Why:** "human" is contextual; a casual README is as wrong as a stiff sales page.
- **Apply:** natural for *this* format — terse microcopy, explanatory docs, voiced email.

## Principle, not property
This distills shared, widely-published observations about how AI writing reads and how
editors fix it. Credit the lineage of an idea where natural; never reproduce a source's
prose or examples. The writing you produce should be your own.
