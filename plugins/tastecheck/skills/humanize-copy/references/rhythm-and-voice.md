# Humanize Copy — Rhythm, Structure & Voice

Vocabulary is the obvious tell; rhythm and stance are the deeper ones. A draft can be
buzzword-free and still scream "AI" because every sentence is the same length, nothing
takes a position, and it's all generically true. Read this for structural rewrites.

## Burstiness: the rhythm signal

"Burstiness" = variation in sentence length and structure. Human writing is jagged;
AI writing is flat — sentences clustering at 15–20 words, all Subject-Verb-Object,
paragraphs shaped like perfect rectangles. Low burstiness lulls the reader into skim
mode where nothing sticks, and it's what detectors and editors flag.

**Fix — the high-low technique:** follow a long, complex, explanatory sentence with a
short, sharp punch. The contrast creates emphasis and rhythm. Like this.

Checks:
- No run of **3+ sentences of similar length**. Break it.
- No three consecutive sentences with the same opening structure.
- Vary paragraph length too — a one-line paragraph is a legitimate tool.

## Sentence-shape variety

- **Fragments** for emphasis. Used sparingly. On purpose.
- **Start with "But" or "And"** when it links the thought — it's natural speech, not
  an error.
- **Cut adverbs** ("very", "really", "quite", "actually") — they pad without adding.
- **One idea per sentence.** AI crams clauses; humans break them apart.

## Structure tells

- **The intro-three-points-conclusion rectangle.** AI defaults to a tidy essay shape.
  Real pieces start in the conflict and end on a thought or action, not a recap.
- **The "Bold lead: + sentence" listicle.** Every bullet formatted identically is a
  tell. Mix bullet styles; sometimes dissolve a 3-item list into a sentence.
- **The rule-of-three everywhere.** "Fast. Simple. Powerful." is fine once. In every
  section it's robotic.
- **The first-10% rule.** Delete the polite warm-up paragraph. The real opening is
  usually the second one — the sentence with an actual claim or tension in it.

## Voice & stance

RLHF trains models toward harmless, balanced, neutral output. That neutrality is a
tell and it kills authority.

- **Take a side.** When the reader wants a recommendation, give one. Balanced
  pros-and-cons on everything signals a writer afraid to be wrong.
- **Drop the hedges.** "It's important to consider", "it could be argued",
  "generally speaking", "aims to" — all signal uncertainty. Commit.
- **Be specific to the point of risk.** Real numbers, named tools, dates, grainy
  details. Specificity is the strongest human signal because the average AI sentence
  is generically true. ("Cuts deploy time from 40 minutes to 6" >> "speeds up
  deployment.")
- **Have a point of view that could alienate someone.** Resonance requires risk.
  Copy that 90% love and 10% reject beats copy everyone finds inoffensive.

## The human sandwich workflow

The reliable way to use AI for prose without shipping its accent:

**Human strategy → AI draft → Human edit.**

If the human isn't both the first step (the angle, the stance, the specifics) and the
last step (cutting tells, varying rhythm, fact-checking), the process is broken. AI is
a good research assistant and first-draft engine, and a poor creative director. Treat
its output as a draft to be cut down, never a final to be published.

## The over-correction trap

Trying too hard to hide AI creates "syntax salad" — grammatically correct sentences
with unnatural word choices (swapping "important" for "paramountly significant"). It
smells like a thesaurus script. Rule: if an edit made a sentence *harder to say aloud*,
revert it. Human ≠ fancy. Human = natural, specific, and varied.

## Register matters
"Humanized" is relative to context. A README should read like a smart engineer
explaining something, not like a sales page. UI microcopy should be terse and plain.
An email should sound like the sender. Match the voice to the medium — don't make
everything casual; make everything *natural for what it is*.
